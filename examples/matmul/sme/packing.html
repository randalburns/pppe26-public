Add-on to Lecture 17 · matrix multiply on the M5's matrix unit

Packing: getting data into the shape the matrix unit wants

The matrix unit is so fast that the slow part of matrix multiply becomes fetching the numbers. Packing is how we feed it: copy the data it is about to use into small buffers, arranged in exactly the order it will read them.

What the matrix unit needs

One SME instruction takes two vectors of 16 numbers and multiplies every number in the first by every number in the second. For C = A × B, those two vectors are a column of A and a row of B, for one value of k.

A vector load reads 16 numbers that sit next to each other in memory. C stores a matrix one row after another. So a row of B is easy, and a column of A is a problem.

The obvious fix costs too much

The simple answer is to transpose A first: make a copy with rows and columns swapped. Then every column of A is a row of the copy, and easy to load.

Transposing touches each number once, n² in all, while the multiply does n³ work. Normally n² is small next to n³. But the matrix unit does the n³ part so quickly that the transpose is no longer small.

Transpose all of A0.35 ms11.1 ms
The best whole multiply1.15 ms10.1 ms

At n = 2048 the transpose alone takes longer than the entire multiply should. Its reads and writes jump 8 KB at a time, and jumps of that size keep landing in the same few slots of the cache. This is the transpose problem from Lecture 6.

Packing: rearrange only what you are about to use

We don't need all of A transposed at once. We need a small part of it, right now, in the right order. So we work on one slab at a time: K values of k. For that slab, we copy the pieces of A and B into two small buffers.

For A, take a block of 32 rows and the K columns of the slab. Copy it so that the 32 numbers for each k sit together. That copy is a small transpose, and it happens entirely in fast cache.

B is already stored the right way round, so it only needs copying. Take the K rows of the slab and cut them into panels 32 columns wide. Each panel becomes its own buffer.

Let the matrix unit do the transposing

Packing A still means transposing, and transposing one number at a time is slow. The matrix unit has a neat way to do it 16 numbers at a time.

A tile is a 16 × 16 grid of numbers stored inside the unit. One instruction loads a row of a tile from memory. Another stores a column of a tile to memory. So: load 16 rows of A into a tile, then store its 16 columns. What comes out is transposed.

What the kernel sees after packing

The multiply itself is now simple. For each 32 × 32 block of C, the kernel walks down one block of Ap and one panel of Bp together. At each step it loads 32 numbers from each, as two vectors of 16, and does four outer products into four tiles.

Did it work?

Two things stand out. Packing in small pieces matters most for big matrices: 3.5 times faster at n = 2048. And how you pack matters as much as whether you pack. The first version packed with ordinary loops, one number at a time, and packing took 30–40% of the run. At n = 1024 it was slower than not packing at all.

How big should a slab be?

Too small is slow. At the start of every slab, each block of C has to be loaded into the tiles, and at the end stored back. With K = 64 that happens 16 times for every block. Fewer, bigger slabs mean less of this.

Too big is slow. The two packed buffers hold n × K numbers each. At n = 2048 and K = 2048 that is 16 MB each, more than the M5's fast shared cache can hold, so the kernel is back to fetching from main memory.

The best slab is the biggest one whose buffers still fit in the cache. As with the tile sizes in Lecture 17, we found it by trying several and measuring.

The whole recipe

  1. Take the next slab: K values of k.
  2. Pack A. For each block of 32 rows, load its K columns into tiles by rows and store them by columns into Ap.
  3. Pack B. Copy the slab's K rows into Bp, one panel for every 32 columns.
  4. For each 32 × 32 block of C: load it into four tiles, add K outer products reading straight down Ap and Bp, and store it back.
  5. Go to the next slab.

Packing spends time copying data in order to save more time reading it. It pays off whenever the arithmetic is fast enough that fetching the numbers is what holds the program back. Math libraries pack for ordinary vector code too; the matrix unit just makes it essential.
